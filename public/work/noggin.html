Stay curious. A place for the thoughts you don’t want to lose.

The problem

Curious thoughts arrive when you can’t stop to look them up. They get forgotten, scattered across notes or lost in open tabs.

Hypothesis

If saving a thought takes seconds and old ones come back gently, people will return to their curiosity instead of losing it, and act on the ones that matter.

How it works

Calm by design: no points, streaks or red counts
  1. 01Catch itSave a Curiosity (an idea) or a Wondering (a question) in seconds, or just say it. No account needed.
  2. 02Meet it againOnce a day at most, an old thought you haven’t opened comes back as a quiet Still curious? card.
  3. 03Act on itBreak it into next steps, or let the AI helper suggest a first one, and send them to Nudge!, where they become tasks.
Curiosities and WonderingsIdeas and questions get their own homes, so neither gets lost in the other.
Still curious?One old thought a day, never more. Open it, snooze it or archive it.
Steps to Nudge!Send next steps as tasks. Signed in, Noggin? shows when each one is done.
AI helpers and Say it (beta)A first step for a Curiosity, a short answer for a Wondering. Or speak a few thoughts and check each one before it’s saved.

The AI helpers

Two optional helpers, off until you switch them on and sign in. Claude suggests one small first step for a Curiosity, or a short answer for a Wondering, in the language you wrote it. Only the thought you’re looking at is sent, with its category: never your notes, other thoughts or your name. Personal questions get two things to weigh and a question back, never a verdict. Answers can be saved to What I found. In Labs (beta), Say it turns up to two minutes of speech into thoughts you check before they’re saved.

The science behind it

The research each feature stands on
Risko and Gilbert, 2016Cognitive offloadingMoving what you need to remember out of your head and into the world frees up attention for what you’re doing now.Save a thought in seconds, typed or spoken, and let it go.
Gruber, Gelman and Ranganath, 2014Curiosity helps memoryPeople remembered answers they were curious about better, even a day later, and curiosity lit up the brain’s reward circuit.Wonderings can get a short answer while the question is still fresh.
Cepeda and colleagues, 2006The spacing effectAcross hundreds of studies, information you come back to after a gap is remembered better than information crammed at once.Still curious? brings one old thought back each day.
